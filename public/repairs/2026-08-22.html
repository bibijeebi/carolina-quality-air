Compressor Safety Relief Valve

Pops at 40 PSI instead of holding to the 175 the tank is rated for

Diagnosis: collapsed spring in the safety pop-off. These are sealed throwaway parts, no rebuild exists. The 175 PSI spec comes straight off the tank's data plate, that number is ground truth. Do not substitute 125, 150, or 200.

Why it matters: this valve is the last line between an overpressure event and a burst tank. Running the compressor with a dead pop-off is the one item on this list that isn't optional.

Husky E106004 · $9.71 · Home Depot (Internet #206485064) Alternate: DEWALT DXCM136-0077 · $34.99 · Tractor Supply
Fix
  1. Drain tank to zero, unit off. Pull the old valve (9/16 or 11/16 flats).
  2. Confirm against the old valve before buying: 1/4 NPT male threads measure ~1/2" across the outside, new body must say 175. The $10 Husky does the identical job as the $35 DEWALT, same 1/4 MNPT 175 ASME spec, and the compressor is not a DeWalt anyway.
  3. 2–3 wraps teflon clockwise, skip the first thread. Hand tight, then 1–2 wrench turns. Brass into steel, don't gorilla it.
  4. Pressure up, soap-test the joint, confirm the pilot unloads around 175.
  5. While drained, crack the bottom drain. Rusty soup means tank crud killed the old valve seat, and the new one eats the same debris unless draining after use becomes habit.

Confidence: ~90% this is the whole fix. A valve that opens at 40 on a 175 system is a failed spring by definition. The 10%: if a brand-new valve also pops early, the valve was never the problem and the regulator/pilot circuit gets looked at next. Failure modes on install: old valve seized in the manifold, or threads turn out 3/8 (that's why the old valve rides along to the store).

ProMaster Mirror: Lower Convex Glass

Passenger side, short-arm mirror. Spotter glass gone entirely.

Diagnosis: glass-only loss. The round clip mount and the 2-wire heater pigtail (white connector, visible dangling in the photo) are intact. That means this is a $20 glass, not the $400 full mirror assembly a dealer quotes.

Why it matters: that convex spotter is the lane-change blind spot coverage on a windowless cargo van. Cheap part, real visibility.

KarParts360 passenger lower heated glass · ~$20 · Amazon L+R pair (fleet spare) · ~$25 · Amazon
Fix
  1. Plug the white heater connector into the leads on the back of the new glass.
  2. Line the round back-plate up with the clip mount, palm-press the center until it clicks. Done.

Confidence: high on the part family; the one check before ordering is van year vs the listing (some listings cut off at 2021) and arm type. This van has standard short arms; any van in the fleet with extended long-arm tow mirrors takes a different SKU. Known quirk from buyer reviews: some aftermarket glass puts the heater leads on the opposite side, extending the wires is trivial.

Same Mirror: Turn Signal Lamp

Lens shattered, housing open to weather. Bulb status unknown.

Diagnosis: the lens is shattered, so the socket sits open to weather. Whether the bulb still works is unconfirmed, and if the van is throwing a turn-signal complaint (fast-flash or dash warning), this lamp is a suspect but not the only one. Water in an open socket kills bulbs and corrodes the connector next, so the housing gets replaced regardless; the replacement units are cheap and ship as one piece with lens and bulb.

Why it matters: an unlit or water-killed turn signal on a company-branded van is an inspection fail and a ticket magnet, on top of just being unsafe on the road.

LONGLING passenger signal lamp w/ bulb · $20–30 · Amazon
Fix (do together with the glass above, same mirror, one teardown)
  1. Free first test: spare bulbs are already in the driver's door pocket. Swap one into this socket and run the blinker. Complaint fixed = circuit and socket are fine, it was just a dead bulb, and the housing swap below becomes weather-proofing instead of urgent. Complaint persists = check the other signal lamps on that side before blaming this one.
  2. Pop the upper mirror glass off: rotate upward and pull firmly with both hands, it snaps off its clip.
  3. Two screws hold the signal assembly. Out with the old.
  4. Bulb is a twist fit with a rubber gasket; use the known-good bulb from the test. Screws in, snap both glasses back.

Confidence: high. This exact swap is documented on the ProMaster owner forums as a 10-minute job, and eBay units marked "Fiat Ducato" fit too because the ProMaster is a rebadged Ducato. Run the blinker after install before buttoning up.

DEWALT DCB102 · 12V/20V MAX DUAL CHARGING STATION

DeWalt DCB102 Dual Charger

Fast-blink fault on every battery, both bays

Diagnosis: fast blink is the "problem pack or charger" code. Isolation is already done: every pack in the fleet faults in both bays of this unit while charging fine on other chargers. That rules out the batteries completely. The charger is the dead part.

Worth fixing? Borderline, and that's fine. Shopped on eBay: brand-new aftermarket DCB102 dual-port clones run $30–36 (listing pictured), and single-bay 12V/20V chargers go for $25–26 if dual isn't a must. At those prices the board-level repair only pays if it's the fuse or a cap, which happens to be the most common failure on this family. Given this unit lives in a truck full of duct dust, conductive grime across the high-voltage traces is also a live suspect, and that fix is free. One trap in the search results: the yellow DC9320 dual charger looks right but is NiCd/NiMH only, it will not charge 20V MAX lithium packs. Skip it.

DCB102 dual-port (new aftermarket) · $30–36 · eBay Single-bay 12V/20V charger · $25–26 · eBay
Bench check, ~30 min
  1. Unplug. Screws are under the rubber feet.
  2. Discharge the main filter cap first, it holds ~300V DC after unplug. Bleed it across a resistor or an insulated-handle screwdriver before touching the board.
  3. Continuity-check the black chemical AC-line fuse near the cord termination.
  4. Visual scan: scorched fusible resistor, bulged or leaking caps, shorted FET on the heat sink.
  5. Isopropyl-bathe the whole board and let it dry. Grime across HV traces has killed more than one jobsite charger.
  6. If the fuse is open, find what killed it before replacing. A bare fuse swap into a shorted FET just blows again.

Confidence: charger-side fault is certain from the isolation logic above. Which component is dead inside is a coin flip until the meter comes out; fuse/cap/grime covers roughly 70% of failures on this charger family per repair writeups. If the bench check strikes out in 30 minutes, stop and buy used, the math doesn't support hunting an oddball transistor.

DEWALT 20V MAX ↔ MILWAUKEE M18 · CROSS-BRAND ADAPTERS

Battery Ecosystem: Mixed DeWalt + Milwaukee Fleet

Can adapters let the two brands share batteries, and does that change the charger math?

Short answer: yes for tools, no for chargers. Third-party adapters exist in both directions: an M18 pack running a DeWalt 20V tool, or a DeWalt pack running an M18 tool. Electrically it's a fitting change, not a conversion, because both platforms are the same 18V nominal under load. DeWalt's "20V MAX" is the no-load reading of the identical cell stack, the number is marketing.

The hard limit, and why it matters for WO-001 item 4: adapters are one-way power bridges to a tool. There is no charging circuit inside one, and putting a battery through an adapter onto any charger risks the pack and the charger. So adapters do not rescue a single battery from the dead DCB102. Charging stays brand-native: DeWalt packs on DeWalt chargers, M18 packs on M18 chargers. The dead dual charger still gets fixed or replaced.

What adapters actually buy the shop: every battery on the truck becomes usable in every tool. A DeWalt pack dying mid-job stops mattering if there's a charged M18 pack in the box, and vice versa. That's real downtime insurance for $15–25 per adapter versus $100+ per extra OEM battery.

M18 battery → DeWalt tool adapter · ~$20 DeWalt battery → M18 tool adapter · ~$20 Same adapters on Amazon · $15–25
Rules of the road
  1. Tool use only. Pull the battery off the adapter before it goes anywhere near a charger, every time.
  2. Prefer the M18-battery→DeWalt-tool direction when there's a choice: M18 packs carry their own low-voltage protection circuit onboard, so the pack protects itself through the adapter. DeWalt packs lean on the tool for that protection, so running one in a Milwaukee tool, stop when the tool noticeably slows instead of flogging it flat.
  3. No fuel gauge passthrough on most adapters, you're flying blind on charge level. Check the pack's own button gauge.
  4. Skip adapters on the highest-draw tools (grinders, big saws under load) and on anything with a semi-enclosed battery bay where the added height won't fit. Drills, drivers, lights, vacuums are the sweet spot.
  5. Buy molded-ABS units with solid copper contacts, not 3D-printed junk. Loose contacts make heat and eat terminals on both ends.

Confidence: high on the mechanics, this is a mature product category with the same physics on every listing. The honest caveat: neither DeWalt nor Milwaukee supports adapters, so a pack damaged while riding one is a warranty conversation nobody wins. Reality check: for a two-brand fleet the adapters are cheap insurance, but they're a supplement to fixing the charger, not a substitute.
