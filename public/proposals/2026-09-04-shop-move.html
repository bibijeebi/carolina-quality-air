Proposal № 009
The Shop at Bessemer Dr

Friday, September 4, 2026 · Close the rented shop, build a cooled workshop behind the house · Phased, mostly in-house labor
Bottom line The lot at 3215 Bessemer Dr is 1.21 acres, zoned RR in unincorporated Pitt County, roughly 155 feet wide at the road and 430 feet deep, with the house sitting mid-lot and about 180 feet of open yard behind it. A 30x40 workshop fits behind the house on the east side without touching the trees. Two things decide whether this is a $35,000 project or a $70,000 one: how much of it we build ourselves, and whether it gets a slab or a gravel pad. One thing decides whether it is allowed at all, and it is not the building. County zoning treats a 1.21-acre RR lot as a residence with a personal workshop, not a contractor's yard. Built and used as Jeff's shop, with gear stored inside and crews meeting at jobs the way they do now, it is a normal accessory building. Put a sign on it and muster twelve people there every morning and it becomes a zoning complaint waiting for a neighbor. The proposal below is the version that works.

2 · What the county allows on it

From the Pitt County Zoning Ordinance (adopted Nov 2019, current edition):

  1. Accessory buildings are permitted by right in RR. Any size. Under 600 sq ft they can sit 5 feet off a side or rear line; 600 sq ft and up they use the principal building setbacks: 10 feet from side and rear property lines, 30 feet from an interior subdivision road. Setbacks grow one foot for every foot of height over 15 feet, so a 16-foot peak means 11-foot side setbacks. On a 155-foot-wide lot none of that bites.
  2. "Contractors" is not a permitted use in RR. The use table allows contractor shops in RA and the commercial districts, not RR. The county's workaround for exactly this situation, the Rural Family Occupation, requires a 2-acre tract. This lot is 1.21.
  3. Home occupation is permitted by right, capped at 25% of the dwelling's floor area whether it is in the house or in an accessory building: about 468 sq ft on this house. It cannot generate traffic, noise, or fumes beyond what the neighborhood already has.
  4. What that means in practice. The building is Jeff's workshop and garage. Company gear lives in it the way it lives in the vans today, the box truck and vans park there the way they do today, and repairs, gear resets, and inventory happen inside it. What it cannot be is a place with a company sign, a daily crew muster, customers, or equipment stored outside. The Cary office is the company address; this is where the owner keeps his stuff.
  5. Two records to read before anything is drawn. The Glen Abbey covenants (recorded with the plat at the Register of Deeds, Grimesland Township, Section 2 was recording lots as recently as 2019) may set a maximum outbuilding size or require matching siding; every neighbor on the aerial has an outbuilding, so they are clearly allowed, but the limits matter for a 30x40. And the septic permit on file at Pitt County Environmental Health shows where the drainfield and repair area are. Nothing gets built over either.

3 · The building

A 30x40 with 12-foot walls, 1,200 sq ft, is the size that fits the lot, the budget, and the actual need: three vans' worth of gear on shelving along one wall, a bench and repair area, the compressor and pressure washer with room to service them, and one bay to pull a van inside out of the weather. Bigger than that and the price per foot stops dropping while the covenant risk goes up.

Gravel pad + tube-steel building

The eastern-NC standard. Carolina Carports and the other regional builders deliver and install a 30x40x12 vertical-roof, fully enclosed steel building on a prepared gravel pad, two roll-up doors and a walk door, engineered for the coastal wind zone. Cheapest way to a dry, lockable shop. Floor is gravel or a later slab.

Concrete slab + the same building

Adds a reinforced 4-inch slab with turned-down edges under the same building. Cleaner, level for benches and lifts, and it is what makes it a real shop instead of a nice barn. Adds roughly $8,000 to $12,000 and a concrete crew; the slab is the one line that is not worth doing ourselves.

4 · Cooling it, which is the whole point

The current shop is miserable in summer for one reason: an uninsulated metal box in Pitt County is a solar oven. Cooling it is two jobs, and the first one is not the air conditioner.

  1. Insulate before you cool. Closed-cell spray foam on the walls and roof (about 2 inches) or rigid foam board with a radiant barrier. Without it a mini split runs all day and loses. With it, 1,200 sq ft needs about two tons.
  2. Two ductless mini splits, 12,000 BTU each, one at each end, or a single 24,000 BTU unit with two heads. Two units means the shop still has AC when one is being serviced. Heat pumps, so winter is covered too. DIY-rated units with pre-charged line sets are what go in under the owner's mechanical permit, with Ben doing the work alongside Jeff (Ben's EPA 608 covers the refrigerant side); a standard contractor-grade unit means a licensed HVAC contractor does the vacuum and charge, and costs about three times as much installed.
  3. Power. A 100-amp subpanel in the shop fed from the house panel, buried aluminum feeder in conduit across the yard, LED high bays, 120V outlets on every wall, two 240V circuits (mini splits, compressor). Under North Carolina law an owner can do electrical work on their own property under a homeowner's permit; anything beyond that is a licensed electrician. Pitt County inspects it either way.
  4. Air movement. A big ceiling fan and gable exhaust so the doors can stay open in spring and fall without running the AC.

6 · What it is worth

The math is the rent plus the drive. Whatever the current shop costs per month, add the 30-minute round trip every time someone needs something from it, times every van, times every week, and that number is what the building saves before it does anything else. A shop that gets used because it is fifty steps from the back door is also a shop where gear gets fixed instead of tossed, which is the entire van-reset program in one sentence. And as a permitted accessory building on the deed it adds to the value of the house; the rented shop adds nothing to anything.

8 · How it goes

  1. Yes in principle, no money. Jeff says the idea is real. Ben pulls the Glen Abbey covenants and the septic permit, walks the yard with Jeff, and stakes the footprint. If the covenants cap outbuildings under 1,200 sq ft or the drainfield is in the way, the page gets rewritten before a dollar moves.
  2. Three quotes. Two tube-steel builders and one red-iron kit supplier for a 30x40x12 enclosed, with and without slab, delivered to 27858. Real numbers replace the ranges above.
  3. Decision one: pad and building. The slab question is decided here. Permits pulled. Building installed. At this point the shop is dry, lockable, and usable, and the rented shop can be emptied into it.
  4. Decision two: power and cooling. Subpanel, insulation, mini splits, done in-house over a few weekends before next summer.
  5. Give notice on the rented shop the month the building is standing, not before.

9 · Reality check

The zoning line is the honest risk. Nothing about a workshop behind a house in Glen Abbey is unusual, and the county does not inspect what is inside a man's shop. What draws a complaint is the outside: a lot of trucks, a lot of people, early mornings, anything stored in the yard. Keep the fleet count at the house what it is now, keep the yard clean, no sign, and this is an accessory building like the neighbors'. If the company ever wants a real yard with the vans and the crew mustering in one place, that is a 2-acre RR lot or an RA or commercial parcel, and it is a different proposal.
