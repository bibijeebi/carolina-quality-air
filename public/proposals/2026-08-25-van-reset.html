Proposal № 003
Weekly Van Reset

Tuesday, August 25, 2026 · Fleet readiness, one flat day per week · Decision needed: yes/no on the standing day
Bottom line One standing day per week — Benny and Bekah, one full day’s hours each at Benny’s rate — and every van starts the week stocked, organized, and inventoried. What CQA is buying is the state of the vans Monday morning, not the hours on a clock. The first few resets will be genuine full days because there’s a system to build; later ones will be shorter because the system holds. The price stays flat either way, because what it’s actually replacing is the thing that never shows up on a timesheet: a crew driving across town mid-job for a part the van should have had.

1 · The problem

The vans run near-full and nobody owns their state. Gear that’s needed is missing or buried, broken rods ride around next to good ones, consumables run out on the job instead of on a shelf, and the fix every time is the same: somebody stops working, drives to a store or back to the shop, and the whole crew’s day stretches. That run costs three ways at once — the driver’s paid hours, the crew standing slower without him, and the job finishing later. On out-of-town work it’s worse, because “the store” might be thirty minutes away in a town nobody knows.

None of that cost is visible on paper. It just leaks out of every week.

2 · What the reset day covers

  1. Inventory every van against a standing checklist. Each van gets a printed list of what lives in it and where. The weekly pass checks the van against its list, not against memory.
  2. Restock consumables. Bags, zip ties, filters, tape, sanitizer, PPE, fuel cans topped — whatever burned down during the week goes back to full before Monday.
  3. Pull broken gear and handle it. Damaged rods, dead tools, and worn parts come out of circulation, get quarantined, and get repair or replacement orders placed the same day — so parts arrive during the week instead of becoming next week’s emergency.
  4. Reset the load. Everything back in its place per the load order: fixed anchors stay put, boxes labeled and staged, straps resident, ladders and cans where they go. Any tech opens any van and knows where everything is.
  5. Flag the pattern. If the same item runs short three weeks running, the standing stock level changes. The checklist is a living document; shortages stop repeating.

3 · Why a flat day and not clocked hours

Because the deliverable is binary: the vans are ready or they aren’t. Early on this is honestly a full day of work for two people — building checklists, labeling, containerizing, doing the first real inventory the fleet has had. Once the system is in place, a reset might take half that. Paying by the clock would punish us for building a system that works fast, and it would tempt the work to sprawl to fill the day. Paying flat means CQA buys an outcome and we’re motivated to make it efficient.

The value math doesn’t change with the hours anyway. One avoided supply run saves roughly two to three crew-hours plus the job overrun; a disorganized fleet produces several of those a week. If the reset day prevents even one or two, it pays for itself before lunch — and the ordering-ahead piece (step 3) prevents the bigger version, where a job stalls waiting on a part nobody knew was broken.

4 · Who does it

Two people, on purposeVan work is genuinely faster with two — one calls the checklist and logs orders, one moves gear, and heavy items don’t wait. Bekah runs the cataloging, ordering, and inventory side; Benny runs the gear, load, and repair-triage side. Both at Benny’s rate for the day. This is also the arrangement already discussed for keeping the take-home van squared away — this proposal just extends it to the fleet, where the payoff is bigger.

5 · What happens on “go”

  1. Pick the standing day. Whatever day the vans are reliably at the shop — likely Saturday or Sunday so nothing competes with jobs.
  2. Reset one: the deep pass. Full inventory of every van, checklists written, broken gear pulled and orders placed, labels and boxes in. This is the full-day one.
  3. Weekly cadence from there. Check, restock, reset, order, done. The checklist gets tighter every pass.
  4. Standing report if wanted. A one-page “fleet status” note after each reset — what was missing, what got ordered, what’s on watch — so the state of the vans is visible without walking out to them.

6 · The honest version

Some weeks we’ll work the full day. Some weeks we won’t, because the system will be holding and the reset will be quick. That’s the point, not a catch — the flat day is the price of never again losing a job morning to a missing part. If the vans stop being ready, the deal isn’t working and it stops. Easiest performance review there is: open a van door Monday morning.
