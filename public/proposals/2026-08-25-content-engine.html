Proposal № 004
Social Media & Content Engine

Tuesday, August 25, 2026 · Online presence + job-site content, one system · Decision needed: green light + ~$125 in gear
Bottom line This is two halves of one machine. Half one costs nothing: claim and fix the online accounts CQA already owns — the Google listing, Nextdoor, BBB — report the impostor account our own website warns customers about, and start feeding the Facebook page that’s currently posting a few times a year. Half two costs about $125: a chest camera and an in-duct camera that ride along on jobs Benny is already working, with the footage edited into posts and videos by the AI tooling he already runs — zero labor cost, zero time off jobs. The result is a company that shows up online the way a 25-year operation should, feeding every other play on this board: the mailer, the door hangers, and the outreach emails all point people at a presence that right now barely answers back.

2 · What feeds it: camera on the job

Cleaning content is a proven genre. Drain-unblocking channels run by working plumbers sit over 500,000 subscribers with millions of views; pressure washing and rug cleaning have the same giants. Duct cleaning has no dominant creator — search it and you find small company promo channels, nobody owning the lane. The footage that fills it is footage we generate every working day and currently throw away.

  1. Chest camera rolls while Benny works. No extra time on the job, no crew disruption. Three planned shots per job beats hours of raw tape: the reveal, the cleaning pass, the after.
  2. The in-duct camera gets the money shot. The signature image of this whole genre is inside the duct looking back as the whip comes through, and the before/after crawl down a dirty trunk. A $25 inspection camera owns that shot.
  3. AI does the editing. Benny’s existing tooling cuts the footage into Facebook posts, Instagram reels, and YouTube videos. No editor gets hired, no agency gets a retainer, no evenings get burned. The marginal cost of a finished post is effectively zero.
  4. Everything double-dips. Every clip is also a Google Business photo upload (which feeds the review push), proof-of-work images for the website, and before/afters for the encapsulation sell. Local uploads get titled to the same neighborhoods the mailer and door hangers hit, so the content and the print program point at the same streets.

3 · The filming rules

Consent doctrineSimple and conservative. Government and secure sites: never — no cameras at jails, courthouses, schools. Occupied homes: only with a signed one-liner — a laminated card and a one-line media release turns it into a checkbox, and no address or face ever appears in a post. Vacant units are the sweet spot: property-manager turnovers and restoration work have no occupant, the dirtiest ducts in the business, and a business contact who’s usually glad to trade filming for the before/after photos we’d hand over anyway. Anything Jeff wants off-camera stays off-camera, full stop.

4 · The money, honestly

The drain-channel guys and the farm cab-cam channels people point at do earn real ad money — but from long-form videos with a mature multi-year catalog, not from a channel in month three. So the honest ranking of what this returns, floor to ceiling:

  1. Floor, immediate: presence and leads. Claimed listings, a Google profile that grows past 7 reviews, an active Facebook, an Instagram that exists, the impostor reported. This is what customers check before calling, and what every mailer QR and outreach email lands on.
  2. Middle, months in: local gravity. Neighborhood-titled videos, Nextdoor answers, review momentum. The stuff that makes CQA the name that comes up when a Facebook group asks “anyone know a duct cleaner?”
  3. Ceiling, year two: the channel itself pays. If the catalog compounds the way the drain channels did, ad revenue, gear-brand sponsorships, and licensing become real. We treat this as upside, not the plan. Year one ad money is beer money and we say so up front.

6 · What happens on “go”

  1. Week 1, the free half. Claim the Google listing and Nextdoor page, hunt down and report the impostor account, fix the BBB category, clean the duplicate directory listings, create the Instagram. One photo dump from the office (any job photos on anyone’s phone) seeds the first month of posts.
  2. Week 1, the camera. Buy the kit, first filmed job is the next suitable one on the schedule — ideally a vacant-unit or PM job so consent is a non-issue.
  3. Weeks 2–4, manual cadence. Two to three posts a week across Facebook, Instagram, and Google Business, run by hand while we learn what lands. First short videos go up.
  4. Steady state. Posting runs on a queue, filming runs on a shot list, and Jeff gets a one-page monthly scoreboard: followers, reviews, post reach, calls that mention seeing us online. If the numbers are flat after a quarter, we say so on that page.

7 · Reality check

Social media doesn’t book jobs by itself any more than a sticker does. What it does is stop losing the ones already in motion: the mailer recipient who checks the Google reviews before calling, the Nextdoor thread that recommends a competitor because we never answered, the customer the impostor account intercepts. The whole ask is ~$125 and permission — the labor is footage from work already being done and editing done by machines. Worst case, CQA ends up with claimed listings, a real Instagram, an impostor reported, and a camera. There is no version of this where the money is wasted.
