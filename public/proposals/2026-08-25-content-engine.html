2 · Prove the filming and editing workflow

  1. One suitable job, using a phone. Obtain the required permission. Capture a steady before shot, useful cleaning action and a matching after shot. Add a short explanation and a clean van or equipment shot.
  2. One 30–45-second first cut. Use the available editing tools to select clips, add captions and CQA branding, and export a vertical social version. Make a horizontal version and thumbnail where useful.
  3. Review before publication. Benny and the designated company reviewer check accuracy, consent, visible details and the contact number. Record capture, editing and review time so the real cost is known.
  4. Buy the camera only if it improves the workflow. A chest camera can help hands-free capture; an inspection camera can help in-duct views. Test footage quality, lighting, mounting and export compatibility before committing.

3 · The filming rules

Consent doctrineSimple and conservative. Government and secure sites: never — no cameras at jails, courthouses, schools. Occupied homes: only with a signed one-liner — a laminated card and a one-line media release turns it into a checkbox, and no address or face ever appears in a post. Vacant units are the sweet spot: property-manager turnovers and restoration work have no occupant, the dirtiest ducts in the business, and a business contact who’s usually glad to trade filming for the before/after photos we’d hand over anyway. Anything Jeff wants off-camera stays off-camera, full stop.

4 · The money, honestly

The drain-channel guys and the farm cab-cam channels people point at do earn real ad money — but from long-form videos with a mature multi-year catalog, not from a channel in month three. So the honest ranking of what this returns, floor to ceiling:

  1. Floor, immediate: presence and leads. Claimed listings, Google profiles with verified baselines and a consistent review request process, an active Facebook, an Instagram that exists, the impostor reported. This is what customers check before calling, and what every mailer QR and outreach email lands on.
  2. Middle, months in: local gravity. Neighborhood-titled videos, Nextdoor answers, review momentum. The stuff that makes CQA the name that comes up when a Facebook group asks “anyone know a duct cleaner?”
  3. Ceiling, year two: the channel itself pays. If the catalog compounds the way the drain channels did, ad revenue, gear-brand sponsorships, and licensing become real. We treat this as upside, not the plan. Year one ad money is beer money and we say so up front.

6 · What happens on “go”

  1. Week 1, the free half. Confirm account ownership, reconcile contact details, investigate duplicates and identify any impersonating account. Correct or create profiles only after checking what already exists. One photo dump from the office (any job photos on anyone’s phone) seeds the first month of posts.
  2. Week 1, the pilot. Film one suitable job with a phone after permission is recorded, including for vacant or property-managed units. Review the first cut before deciding whether to buy the camera kit.
  3. Weeks 2–4, manual cadence. Two to three posts a week across Facebook, Instagram, and Google Business, run by hand while we learn what lands. First short videos go up.
  4. Steady state. Posting runs on a queue, filming runs on a shot list, and Jeff gets a one-page monthly scoreboard: followers, reviews, post reach, calls that mention seeing us online. If the numbers are flat after a quarter, we say so on that page.

7 · Reality check

Social media doesn’t book jobs by itself any more than a sticker does. What it does is stop losing the ones already in motion: the mailer recipient who checks the Google reviews before calling, the Nextdoor thread that recommends a competitor because we never answered, the customer uncertain which company account is official. Start with permission, account access and one phone-footage pilot. Judge the result against actual time spent, usable footage and customer response before adding equipment or a posting commitment.
