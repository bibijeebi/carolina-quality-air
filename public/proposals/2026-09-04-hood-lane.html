Proposal № 007
Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning

Friday, September 4, 2026 · Two decisions: credential now (~$1,070), equipment after the first quotes · Recurring restaurant work under NFPA 96
Bottom line Every commercial kitchen in North Carolina is required by the state fire code to have its exhaust system cleaned from the hood to the rooftop fan on a fixed schedule, monthly to yearly depending on what they cook, by a third party who leaves paperwork the fire marshal accepts. That is a duct cleaning job with grease instead of dust, on a calendar, at restaurants that never stop cooking. Wake County's own inspection data shows 408 restaurants with hood or exhaust violations on record, 83 of them repeat offenders. The credential the industry recognizes is IKECA, which CQA can hold for about $1,070 this year with no prerequisites. The only real equipment gap is a hot-water pressure washer. This page lays out what the work is, what it pays, what it takes, and a two-step way in that does not commit to the equipment until the first quotes are real.

1 · What the work is

NFPA 96 is the national standard for commercial cooking ventilation. North Carolina adopts it through the NC Fire Prevention Code, so it is enforceable by every local fire marshal in the state. The part that creates a market is Table 11.4, the inspection and cleaning schedule:

Cooking operationFrequencyExamples
Solid fuel (wood, charcoal)MonthlyWood-fired pizza, BBQ, steakhouses with charcoal grills
High volumeQuarterly24-hour operations, charbroiling, wok cooking, heavy frying
Moderate volumeSemiannualMost sit-down restaurants
Low volumeAnnualChurches, day camps, seasonal kitchens, senior centers

A compliant job is the whole path: hood and filters, plenum, every duct section to bare metal, the rooftop fan (often on a hinge kit so it can be tipped), then a service sticker on the hood, tags at every access panel opened, and a written report with photos to the owner. Fire marshals in NC generally want that report from a third-party provider, not a note from the kitchen manager. Insurance carriers pull the same records after a fire.

The work itself: hang plastic containment over the cookline and floor, foam degreaser on everything, let it dwell, hot-water pressure wash from the fan down so the grease runs to a catch, wet-vac the runoff, scrape what the water will not move, reinstall filters, photograph before and after. Nights and early mornings, because the kitchen has to be cold and the restaurant closed. A single-hood job is a three to four hour crew visit. Multi-hood systems with long duct runs are a full night.

2 · Why this fits CQA

  1. It is the same skill set. Ductwork, access panels, rooftop fans, containment, before-and-after documentation, night work. Crews already know how to open a system, clean it end to end, and prove it. The differences are grease chemistry, hot water instead of vacuum, and a fire-code report instead of a NADCA one.
  2. We have done it. The Logan's job was a post-fire, never-cleaned system, the worst case the trade has. Maintained accounts on a quarterly or semiannual schedule are a fraction of that effort, which is the whole point of the contract model: the second visit is always easier than the first.
  3. The customers are already identified. Wake County publishes restaurant inspection data. The pull in Lead Report № 004 found 408 facilities with hood or exhaust findings, 83 with repeat findings. Those are restaurants a fire marshal has already told to fix this. The pull is documented in Lead Report № 004.
  4. It is recurring by law. Duct cleaning is a once-every-few-years sale. Hood cleaning is quarterly or semiannual on a schedule the fire code sets, so one closed account is four to eight visits a year until they close or churn. Twenty maintained accounts is a route, not a lead list.
  5. It opens doors we already knock on. Restoration subs (Servpro, Sasser), facility maintenance directors, the multifamily companies with clubhouse kitchens, hotels, schools, hospitals, and the base dining facilities all buy hood cleaning. Being the vendor who does both duct and hood is a reason to keep us on the list.

4 · What it takes

The credential

No state or national license is required to clean hoods in North Carolina. The credential customers, fire marshals, and insurers recognize is IKECA (International Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning Association), the hood-world equivalent of NADCA. Verified from IKECA's January 2026 candidate handbook and the membership policy amended August 27, 2026:

ItemFact
Who can testAnyone. No experience prerequisite, no company sponsorship, no membership required to sit or hold the cert.
LevelsCECT (technician) and CECS (specialist, the one member companies must employ). Both 100 questions, 2 hours, closed book, 70% to pass, at a Meazure Learning test center or by remote proctor from home.
CECS exam fee$395 for an employee of a member company, $750 for an individual non-member. Retakes $100, two allowed within a year.
RenewalEvery June 1. $120 member / $175 non-member, plus 1.5 CEUs a year (NADCA conferences count).
Company membershipIntroductory (first year, one time): $575 dues + $100 application. Active North American after that: $995 a year, $2M liability and workers' comp on file, one CECS on staff. Introductory members cannot use the IKECA logo yet; Active members can.
The 90-day ruleA company can join before it has a certified employee and gets 90 days to produce one, testing candidates at the member rate during that window. That is what makes the $395 path work.
Conference requirementIntroductory members must send one person to one IKECA conference in the membership year. The next one is the Fall Technical Seminar, October 28–30, 2026, Hyatt Regency Greenville SC, about a four-hour drive. Worth up to 2.0 CEUs.

Ben would sit the CECS. The study path is the same as the ASCS prep that just worked: the IKECA study guide plus a drill site built from it. The exam is written for people with field knowledge, so plan on the study guide plus at least a few ride-alongs or a training job before the test date.

Insurance: hood work is a covered class on most general liability policies but should be confirmed with the carrier before the first job, since the fire exposure is the whole reason customers buy it.

5 · How to get in without betting the equipment

  1. Credential first, ~$1,070. CQA files the IKECA Introductory application ($675), Ben registers for the CECS at the member rate ($395) inside the 90-day window, studies through October, tests before the Fall Technical Seminar so the trip in late October doubles as CEUs and the first look at the trade's vendors and used-equipment market.
  2. Quote before buying. With the cert in hand, work the 408-restaurant list and the restoration and facilities contacts already on the board. Ten real quotes tell us whether the pricing band above holds in Raleigh and Greenville. No pressure washer until two or three accounts have said yes.
  3. Buy the machine on the first signed account. Used if a good one is available, new if not. First jobs done with Ben plus one tech, at night, on the maintained-account rate, with the first-clean premium where it applies.
  4. Upgrade membership when the logo matters. Active membership ($995) in year two if hood work is real revenue; it adds the IKECA logo on the van and the site, which restaurants recognize.

6 · What could go wrong

The last hood job was miserable. It was also a fire-damaged system that had never been cleaned, which is not what a maintained account looks like. The honest risks are: night work competing with crew rest, grease disposal (runoff has to go to a grease-tolerant drain or be hauled, not the storm drain), and a crowded local market where Kitchen Guard, the national franchises, and local independents already hold the chains. The answer to the last one is the same as it is in duct work: the independents and the fire-marshal-flagged list, not the chains. If the first ten quotes come back cold, the exposure is the credential money and a trip to Greenville, and the CECS still counts as a second certification on the company's resume for bids.
