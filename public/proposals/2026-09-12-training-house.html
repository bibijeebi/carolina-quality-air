Proposal № 011
The Training House

Saturday, September 12, 2026 · A full residential duct and dryer-vent job at Ben's house with no customer and no clock · Decision needed: pick a Sunday and the crew
Bottom line

Ben's house in Willow Spring is a 2006 one-story on a vented crawl space: horizontal propane furnace under the floor, flex duct, eleven floor registers, two wall returns, and an electric dryer venting out of a laundry room off the garage. It has never been professionally cleaned, there's a St. Bernard in it, and Ben found genuinely loaded runs when he shop-vacced the registers last year. It is the exact house the mailer just went to 2,171 of, and it's ours for a day. The ask is one crew day where the guys work every station at their own pace with no customer and no clock, and Bekah documents all of it. Out of that day, or a lighter second one with just Ben and Bekah, come the before-and-after set, the phone video and the written residential procedure. Cost is the crew's hours plus about $60 in consumables. Ben's own hours are free; the cleaned house is his end of it. Nothing gets invoiced.

2 · Why this house and not a customer's

Three reasons. First, it is the job we sell most and just mailed for: a 2000s-built one-story on a crawl with flex duct and a furnace under the floor. Most of the calls that come off the September mailer will be this house. Second, nobody is paying, so nobody is watching the clock. A new guy can cut his first return cleanout twice. Someone can patch a hole wrong and then patch it right. The camera can be set up and reset between every register. None of that is possible on a Tuesday in Raleigh with a homeowner in the kitchen. Third, it is Ben's, so the before-and-after photos, the crawl-space footage and the mistakes all belong to us and can go straight into the website, the social pilot and the written procedures without asking anybody's permission.

Two things are deliberately not on the menu. There is no fiberglass-lined duct here, so encapsulation stays a commercial-job lesson. And the crawl runs do not get mastic-sealed on this day, because the state rebate program Ben is in may pay a registered contractor to do that in 2027 and we shouldn't do a free version of work the program would cover.

4 · The stations

This is the residential job in the order we actually run it, with what each station teaches. Bekah shoots a before, a during and an after at every one, phone on a tripod for the wide shot and handheld for the close-ups.

  1. Walk and count. Whole crew walks the house before anyone touches a tool. Find the furnace and the crawl hatch, count eleven supplies and two returns, trace which runs feed which rooms, spot the loaded and the suspect runs, photograph every register face before it's touched. This is the step new guys skip and pay for at hour four.
  2. Containment and setup. Plastic under every register and the work paths, corner guards, dog out of the house. Neg-air machine outside the crawl hatch, exhaust pointed away from the house and the neighbors, eight-inch collector hose run through the hatch to the return trunk at the furnace. Routing a stiff hose through a low crawl to a cleanout is its own skill and there is no way to teach it except in a crawl.
  3. Return side first. Both wall grilles and their filters come out. Cut the cleanout at the normal spot on the return trunk, hose on, machine running, whip the return runs back to the trunk. Teaching point: returns are always the nastiest, and they come first so the supply side doesn't get re-dirtied.
  4. Supply side from the boots. Eleven registers, one man per run, rotating. The whip pass per run from the register: half on, knock the boot face, forward all the way up and back, all the way up and drag back in circles, forward up and back. At the trunk, reverse up, air on and pull back, whip up and circles on the way back, while someone holds the vac hose at the cleanout. Soft rod in the flex and around the 90s, hard rod only on straight runs.
  5. Cut, plug and patch, in the crawl. Wherever the whip can't reach from a boot, cut access and then close it right: pop plugs in the one-inch holes, sheet-metal patch on the eight-inch, screws at corners and sides, mastic on the seam and the face, insulation rolled back and FSK tape over it. The run that looked pulled off its collar gets re-hung, re-collared and mastic-sealed at the joint, which is the duct-repair line we already sell. Everyone does one patch on the table before anyone does one on the trunk.
  6. Coil last. The evaporator coil on a horizontal furnace in a crawl is the awkward one, which is why it belongs on a training day. Coil cleaner on the fins, degreaser on the housing, the small coil-cleaning washer, pan and drain checked. It goes last because whipping near the furnace drops debris into the coil, and that order rule is worth saying out loud once.
  7. Grilles and registers at the pressure washer. Tarp down in the driveway, garden hose to the washer, degreaser and a brush on the stubborn ones, laid out to dry in the order they came off so they go back where they belong.
  8. Dryer vent. Pull the dryer, disconnect the transition duct, measure the run from the wall collar to the outside hood and count the elbows. Code allows 35 feet of four-inch duct less five feet per 90 and two and a half per 45, and the outside hood may not have a screen on it (IRC M1502). Clean it with the brush and the vac from the hood end, confirm the damper swings free, put it back with the joints running with the airflow, then run the dryer and feel the flow at the hood. NADCA publishes a residential single-dryer checklist (DEDP) and that becomes our dryer-vent card. This is the add-on the door hangers were drafted around, and the crew should have done one before they offer it.
  9. Fog. Deodorizer fog through the system with the blower on, the way every residential job ends.
  10. Closeout and the customer walk. New MERV 11 filters in the returns (Ben supplies them), registers back on straight, plastic up, floors swept, after photos of every register face next to its before. Then Ben does the Perry walkthrough with Bekah playing the homeowner, on camera: fan on AUTO not ON, pleated filters, why closed doors starve the return. The guys watch a sale being set up, which is the other half of the job.
  11. If the garage mini split is hung by then. Filters, coil, blower wheel and drain on a ductless head, thirty minutes, one more thing we can quote on the residential ticket.

5 · What comes out of it

Training

A crew that has each done the whole residential job once, slowly, on the hardest common access we see, with their own before-and-after photos as proof. New hires from here on train on this house before their first paid job instead of on a customer.

The written procedure

The station list above with photos of each step becomes the written residential procedure, one page with pictures, plus a one-page card for the van, so the job gets run the same way regardless of who's on it.

Content

The shot list: eleven register faces before and after, the hose going into the crawl, the return cleanout cut and patched, one full whip pass from a boot, the coil before and after, the dryer hood with the damper swinging, and the filmed walkthrough. That is the job-site photo set the website still owes and the first real material for the social pilot in № 004, with no homeowner consent to chase and nothing posted until Jeff has seen it.

The dryer-vent line

The door hangers were drafted around a dryer-vent inspection with every duct cleaning. After this day the crew has measured, cleaned and verified one to the code and the NADCA checklist, and the card that comes out of it is what they hand the customer.

7 · How it goes

  1. Jeff picks the format and the Sunday. A or A plus B, and which two to four guys. September 20, September 27 or October 4 all work on Ben's end; the crawl is a better place to learn in the fall than in July.
  2. Ben stages the house. Dog out of the house for the day, cars out of the driveway, crawl hatch cleared, plastic pulled back where the hose runs, new filters bought, camera batteries charged.
  3. The van comes down loaded per the standard load: neg-air machine, compressor, whips and both rod types, portable vac, pressure washer, fogger, patches, plugs, mastic, FSK, plastic, corner guards.
  4. Run the stations in order, slow, everybody on everything. Bekah shoots. Ben keeps the log of what went right, what went wrong and what was missing from the van, the same way the gear days are logged.
  5. Within the week: the written procedure and van card go out, the photo set gets sorted into pairs, and the first cut of the content goes to Jeff to review before anything is posted.

8 · Reality check

The crawl is the crawl. Half of it is a belly crawl and it will be slow, muddy and unpopular, which is exactly why it's worth training in. Expect the day to run long the first time; the point is that nothing is riding on it. The only way this goes wrong is if it gets treated like a job with a customer in the house and everyone rushes, so the lead's job on the day is to slow it down. And if Jeff would rather not spend crew hours on it, B alone costs about $40 and Bekah's morning, and still produces the photos, the procedure and the dryer-vent card, just without the crew having done it themselves.
